# THUMBNAIL DNA — OBSERVED FROM USER-SUPPLIED CHANNEL SCREENSHOTS

This reference summarizes visual patterns visible in the supplied competitor screenshots.

## A. MODERN ABANDONED PROPERTY

Typical visual grammar:

- bright or clean natural daylight
- wide enough to show the **entire specific property**
- protagonist in foreground or midground
- often rear or three-quarter rear arrival pose
- luggage, boxes, old vehicle, or child when story-supported
- uncluttered visual hierarchy
- property is readable at small size
- red arrow sometimes added in post to target a specific structure/window/door

Best use:

- motel
- school
- lakeside property
- workshop
- boathouse
- island house
- farm

Upgrade rule:

Do not force the woman to face away if a face-visible emotional reaction is stronger.
The emotional state and title clue determine pose.

---

## B. FRONTIER / LOST HISTORY

Typical visual grammar:

- wide arid landscape or canyon
- period-dressed woman often viewed from behind
- unique object/place dominates: wagon train, mine, ghost town, lighthouse, railroad, cliff structure
- warm earth tones
- clear sense of scale and isolation
- minimal text-free composition

Best use:

When the title's click engine is the location/object itself.

---

## C. WINTER SURVIVAL / HIDDEN SHELTER

Typical visual grammar:

- dramatic contrast between hostile snow/cold and warm shelter interior
- protagonist actively carrying, digging, bracing, pulling, or protecting
- food, fire, tools, animals, stores, or shelter mechanics visible when supported
- higher visual energy than the quiet-property thumbnails
- strong orange/blue thermal contrast

Best use:

When survival method is central to the title.

---

## D. GOTHIC ESTATE / INHERITANCE

Typical visual grammar:

- large manor/castle/estate occupies much of frame
- overcast or muted historical atmosphere
- woman foreground, often carrying luggage or child
- emotion of exile, uncertainty, or arrival
- architecture itself is mysterious
- arrow sometimes added to a window/door/wing as a post-production cue

Best use:

When inheritance, rejection, or hidden legacy is the primary click engine.

---

## E. DISCARDED-ASSET / RESOURCE TRANSFORMATION

Typical visual grammar:

- saturated daylight
- impossible visual scale of one material/object category
- protagonist actively handling/standing beside it
- very high object density
- immediate “how can this be useful?” question

Transferable lesson:

Use **specificity and scale**, not the aesthetic itself, when the Homeless script does not belong to this family.

# UNIVERSAL CTR RULES

## 1. ONE VISUAL QUESTION

The frame should create one dominant question, not five.

## 2. TITLE OBJECT MUST BE VISIBLE

If the title says motel, show the motel.
If it says railroad car, show the railroad car.
If it says boathouse, show the boathouse.
If it says cellar, show the cellar access or its visual cue.

## 3. PROTAGONIST = EMOTIONAL ANCHOR

Use body language that tells the wound:

- tired arrival
- clutching luggage
- protecting child
- frozen in shock
- resisting wind
- kneeling at clue
- hand on locked door
- looking back after being expelled

## 4. 2-3 ELEMENT HIERARCHY

Preferred:

1. protagonist
2. specific property/object
3. one clue or contextual prop

Avoid crowds, multiple unrelated structures, or decorative clutter.

## 5. NO SPOILER TREASURE

Show the door, key, crack, room entrance, strange object, or reaction.
Do not show the complete final secret unless the title itself reveals it.

## 6. ARROW POLICY

Use arrows as post-production annotation, not as part of the AI base render by default.

If used:
- exactly one arrow
- point to one title-relevant clue
- never point to a random window just to increase CTR

## 7. READABILITY AT SMALL SIZE

Ask:

- Can the exact property be recognized?
- Can the protagonist's emotion/action be read?
- Is the clue distinguishable?

If not, simplify.
